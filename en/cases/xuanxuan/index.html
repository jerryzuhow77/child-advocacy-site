CHAPTER 01 · FIVE SIGNALS

She was not invisible

Publicly available information does not describe a child entirely outside the system. It describes a child who appeared repeatedly within reporting and service channels. Each signal was a lit window; the question is whether anyone read those lights together.

Seeing a child once is not the same as completing protection. Repeated signals must raise the risk level—not reset it.

CHAPTER 02 · CLASSIFICATION

A report placed into a category

Systems need categories, but classification cannot replace judgment. Fields, risk levels, and referrals are only the beginning. Where the child is, who is caring for her, and whether someone has directly verified her safety are answers no form should erase.

Classification can route a process; it must not decide who gets to stop asking questions.

CHAPTER 03 · TWO CITIES

The child was in Taichung; the case returned to Tainan

Cross-jurisdiction cases can fracture between administrative authority and physical reality. Documents may move along an official route, but risk does not pause. The sending and receiving agencies need a named person responsible for closing the loop.

A handoff is complete not when a file is sent, but when someone has accepted it, seen the child, and confirmed immediate safety.

CHAPTER 04 · THE UNFINISHED CHECK

Every procedure moved; protection did not arrive

Reports, routing, referrals, and calls can all leave a record. Yet the purpose of procedure is to bring a child to safety—not simply to bring a document to the next office. Without the final safety check, even a complete administrative trail remains incomplete.

“Actions taken” are not the same as “protection achieved.” Child protection must test outcomes, not only steps.

CHAPTER 05 · ONE RISK PICTURE

Why did five rescue signals never become one answer?

A single piece of information may look fragmentary. When similar concerns recur, they must accumulate into one risk picture. Systems should compare information across reports, agencies, and cities—not leave every worker holding only one small tile.

The missing piece is clear ownership: who combines every warning and answers for the final decision?

CHAPTER 06 · ACCOUNTABILITY

Who is responsible for the final step?

Accountability must go beyond whether a rule was followed. Who confirmed acceptance? Who directly saw the child? Who combined earlier signals? Who escalated a failed handoff—and who verified that the loop was truly closed?

A protection system needs more than boundaries of responsibility. It needs a route where no child can disappear during transfer.
