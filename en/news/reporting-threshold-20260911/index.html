Hearing recollections × Mandatory reporting × September 16 hearing

Must limbs be broken before the reporting threshold is met?

Later, when people recalled the hearings in Kaikai's case, perhaps what stayed with them most was not a passage of testimony but “three teeth.”

In September 2023, large bruises appeared on Kaikai's body; in October, he was markedly listless; by November, three teeth reportedly fell out in a single night.

One abnormality after another appeared, as if the child were knocking again and again for help.

Yet the hearing still discussed procedures, roles, information gathering and supervisory discussion. It also mentioned possible adjustment difficulties for children placed for adoption and a need for clearer evidence to decide whether to report.

Until the judge asked:

“What circumstances, exactly, would make the Child Welfare League Foundation consider that it has a duty to report? Does the child have to be beaten until covered in injuries, with arms and legs broken?”

The proceedings paused for a time.

This question seemed directed not only at one witness but at every adult: when a child has shown abnormalities again and again, how long will we wait before believing that he is asking for help?

Reporting is not a finding of guilt

Under the law, mandated reporters should initiate reporting when mistreatment of a child or young person is “suspected.” Reporting enables subsequent investigation and protection mechanisms; it does not require the reporter to first decide who is guilty.

Kaikai could not say, “I am in pain.” His bruises, exhaustion and three lost baby teeth could only speak for him.

But by the time adults finally viewed these signals together, the child no longer had a chance to speak.

That question still deserves to be remembered: must we really wait until a child is covered in injuries, with broken limbs, before reporting?
