DAILY CHILD PROTECTION BRIEF · 2026-09-12

From addictive design to accountable safety claims

California legislation and a Texas TikTok ruling: child online safety requires verifiable outcomes, not labels.

AI autumn flower-and-bird ink-wash concept; not an event photograph or an artist's original.

Two US developments address one core issue. Platforms cannot maximise minors' attention through recommendation and autoplay while relying on labels such as “Restricted Mode” to claim that risk is controlled. California moved duties into law; a Texas court examined whether TikTok's safety claims matched actual performance.

Developments and procedural status

California, USState law10 Sep 2026Signed into law

California signs a 13-bill child online-safety package

Confirmed: The Governor's office announced 13 signed bills. AB 1709 restricts covered platforms from offering certain addictive features to users under 16, including autoplay and algorithmic feeds based on a user's history or profile. Other bills address child-safety audits and annual risk assessments for companion chatbots, parental controls, crisis protocols, pupil data, and digitally altered or AI-generated child sexual exploitation material.

Boundary: Signature does not mean every duty takes effect immediately. Commencement dates, implementing rules, age-assurance methods and legal challenges require bill-by-bill tracking. Privacy and free-expression objections reported by Reuters are stakeholder positions, not judicial findings.

System warning

Age assurance can create privacy risks if it requires excessive identity collection; weak safeguards can leave children exposed. Regulators should audit both data minimisation and protective effectiveness.

Advocacy impact

Taiwan could require disclosure of default settings for minors, recommendation controls, crisis-response times, parental notifications, independent audits and child-impact assessments.

Texas, USConsumer-protection law10 Sep 2026Liability found; remedies pending

Texas court finds TikTok's child-safety claims misleading

Court finding: A Texas judge found that TikTok's representations about content filtering and Restricted Mode violated state consumer-protection law. The official release and Reuters reported that some content represented as removed or restricted—including nudity, drugs, alcohol, injury and profanity—remained accessible to minors.

Pending: Proceedings continue on relief and penalties. Liability was found on specified safety representations; not every issue has reached final judgment. The Attorney General's description is the claimant agency's position, while TikTok's full response and further review remain to be confirmed.

System warning

Having a named safety feature is not proof that it works. Platforms should preserve activation rates, false positives, missed detections, actual exposure and complaint-resolution times.

Advocacy impact

Claims that harmful content is filtered should be independently reproducible through disclosed test methods, version histories and third-party results.

Six auditable questions

  1. Are autoplay, addictive personalised feeds and contact from strangers disabled by default for minors?
  2. How is age assessed without excessive identity collection?
  3. Are guardians notified when safety settings are disabled?
  4. Are Restricted Mode's miss rate, false-positive rate and test samples published?
  5. How quickly do crisis protocols start after self-harm, exploitation or grooming signals?
  6. Are safety features independently retested after product changes?
Child online safety is not another switch in the interface. It is proof that risk was reduced and warning signs were acted upon.
