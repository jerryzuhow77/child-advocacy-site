More reports, but children still left in danger: placement warnings from South Korea’s 38 child-abuse deaths

Do more reports lead to timely protection? South Korea’s official 2025 figures expose five decision points that Taiwan’s out-of-home-care system must make auditable.

2025 SOUTH KOREA

Official Korean data: more reports do not mean less danger

South Korea recorded 63,166 reports in 2025, up 25.7%. Confirmed abuse cases totalled 23,648, down 3.4%. Reporting volume and substantiated cases must therefore be kept distinct.

SYSTEM COMPARISON

The shared question: when must a child be removed?

South Korea: separation and repeat-abuse warnings

  • 2,043 confirmed cases (8.6%) resulted in separated care, including 1,343 immediate separations.
  • 3,584 cases (15.2%) involved repeat abuse.
  • Thirty-eight children died, eight more than in 2024; 18 were under one year old.
  • The government plans risk-indicator safety checks and a special committee to analyse suspected abuse deaths.

Taiwan: legal authority exists, but handoffs can fail

  • Article 56 authorises emergency placement when a child lacks proper care, faces severe harm or needs immediate protection. Timely escalation remains the operational question.
  • Audit evidence shows that some children already receiving protection services later reached specialist medical centres with serious or complex injuries.
  • Family-based capacity, reliance on institutions, placement moves and quality oversight remain pressure points.
  • Return-home and closure decisions need verified safety changes, not merely completed services or an absence of new reports.

FIVE HANDOFFS

Five interfaces Taiwan should make auditable

01｜Escalate

Set deadlines for supervisory review and renewed visits when sources conflict.

02｜Immediate safety

Assess present danger separately from the risk of deterioration.

03｜Removal decision

Record reasons, counter-evidence and the reviewing official.

04｜Care quality

Publish capacity, waits, placement moves and harm occurring in care.

05｜Fatality review

Run de-identified, cross-agency reviews and track implementation.

READ THE NUMBERS

Four figures that should not be conflated

63,166 reports

Signals entering the system, including cases not later substantiated. The 25.7% rise may also reflect greater public awareness.

23,648 substantiated

41.0% of accepted reports and 844 fewer than in 2024. More reports did not mean more substantiated cases.

2,043 separations

8.6% of substantiated cases, including 1,343 immediate separations. This is an observed distribution, not an ideal target.

3,584 repeat cases

15.2% met the official repeat-abuse definition: a substantiated case in the prior five years and again in 2025.

The highest-risk combination

Parents accounted for 85.3% of perpetrators and homes for 83.5% of locations; 18 of 38 deaths involved infants under one. Systems cannot wait for self-disclosure or another obvious injury.

TAIWAN REFORM CHECKLIST

Six auditable indicator groups

Time

From report to direct contact, medical assessment, supervisory review and necessary removal.

Contact quality

Unannounced visits, private contact, home inspection and record triangulation.

Decision trail

Counter-evidence, reviewer and next review date for non-removal or return.

Capacity waits

Vacancies, wait times and out-of-area placements by care type.

Stability and harm

Placement moves, harm in care and complaint outcomes.

Return outcomes

Re-reporting, reopening and replacement at 3, 6 and 12 months.

Seven supervisory questions

  1. Was the child seen directly and able to communicate safely?
  2. Are age, injury, delayed care or information control increasing vulnerability?
  3. What objective records resolved conflicting accounts?
  4. If the child stays home, who owns each safety action?
  5. What deadline or failure automatically triggers escalation?
  6. Does the placement fit the child rather than merely offer a bed?
  7. Does return or closure reflect reduced risk or completed paperwork?

These indicators are this site’s advocacy proposal—not a verbatim Korean policy or an existing Taiwan-wide performance standard.
