Appeal tomorrow · hearing summary

February 23 hearing summary: four warning-sign groups

Warning sign 1 | Repeated injuries and “self-hitting / self-falling”

Hearing summary / public coverage, not a verbatim transcript

Summary: Public coverage indicates that a caregiver explained unusual injuries as self-hitting or self-falling; Chen said at the hearing that she had trusted the caregiver’s account.

What the appeal should examine: Whether repeated or medically unusual injuries were independently observed and cross-checked with medical confirmation, photographs or third parties, rather than recorded solely from a caregiver’s statement.
Warning sign 2 | Repeated fever and bodily anomalies

Hearing summary / public coverage, not a verbatim transcript

Summary: Hearing coverage and related reports refer to repeated fever and bodily anomalies during care. The key question is whether these facts were assessed cumulatively as risk, rather than closed one by one.

What the appeal should examine: A complete timeline, medical records, follow-up visits, supervisor awareness and escalation. A single “reply received” does not establish that risk was removed.
Warning sign 3 | Bruising and visible injuries

Hearing summary / public coverage, not a verbatim transcript

Summary: Prosecutors have argued that visible injuries were already warning signs in visits and known information; Chen denied or explained that she did not identify a serious risk. The court must weigh both accounts and the underlying records.

What the appeal should examine: Whether injury location, time of observation, photographs, the child’s condition and the caregiver’s explanation matched; and whether visit records explain why higher-level protection was not initiated.
Warning sign 4 | Tooth loss and a medical alert

Hearing summary / public coverage, not a verbatim transcript

Summary: Tooth loss and other anomalies became contested issues in public hearing coverage. A caregiver’s explanation, the social worker’s response and medical images cannot be resolved by a single sentence.

What the appeal should examine: When medical opinion was obtained, whether causes were pursued and records preserved, whether referral or reporting occurred, and how the anomaly changed later visits and placement decisions.

Tomorrow must show more than attitude

Procedure

The accused has a right to defend and to maintain innocence; prosecutorial claims also require proof. The appeal should match every warning sign, visit and non-escalation decision to evidence.

System

This case cannot be reduced to whether one person “saw” something. What must remain is a reviewable record of timestamps, observations, supervisory decisions, medical confirmation and inter-agency handoffs.

Six questions that can no longer be skipped

  1. Who first received information about injury, fever, bruising or tooth loss?
  2. Who verified the source and the medical facts?
  3. What did each visit observe, ask and record?
  4. When should visit frequency, supervisory review or placement have escalated?
  5. Why was cross-agency protection not activated as risk accumulated?
  6. If the same signs appeared today, could the system make a traceable safety decision within 24 hours?
Children do not write their own risk assessments. Every adult statement, record and decision not to escalate should be examined one by one through evidence and due process.
