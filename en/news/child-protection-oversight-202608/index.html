Reports rose by 60%.
Is the safety net really safer?

The Legislative Yuan’s latest research brings surging reports, budget gaps, abuse examinations and judicial intervention into one account of the system. The question is not just how many reports were received, but how many children were seen, investigated and protected in time.

What does this research add?

The Legislative Yuan’s August 2026 study does not attribute child-protection problems solely to frontline staff. It examines reporting volume, cooperation across services, budgets and forensic capacity together. According to the study, child-protection reports increased by more than 60% from 2022 to 2025, while resources did not adequately keep pace with protection needs and emerging risks.

More reports do not mean less risk

Reporting is the gateway to the safety net, not a protection outcome. As reports surge, authorities should disclose how many cases are investigated, how many are not assigned, the time to first contact, the safety measures taken, and whether further reports follow case closure.

Total reporting figures alone cannot show whether an increase reflects better recognition, repeated reports, or risks not addressed in time. Advocacy should therefore move beyond whether a report was made to what happened afterwards.

Three accountability measures to establish now

AssignmentPublish assignment rates by risk level, reasons for non-assignment and review findings.
SpeedPublish the time to first in-person contact, abuse examination and interagency consultation.
OutcomesTrack return home, further reports after closure and repeat victimization, not just service contacts.

Early judicial intervention needs clear thresholds

The study’s call for stronger abuse examinations, forensic capacity and earlier judicial intervention deserves support. But early intervention must be more than a slogan. Written procedures and auditable milestones are needed for when police and prosecutors become involved in serious suspected abuse, who preserves evidence, how health and social services exchange information, and how protection continues below the threshold for criminal proceedings.

  • Establish standards for early consultation and evidence preservation in serious abuse cases.
  • Ensure pediatric, forensic and judicial interviewing practice is trauma-informed and appropriate for children.
  • Document reasons for any decision not to intervene or to delay, and require supervisory review.

Next steps: five questions for government

  1. Following the increase in reports, what are each locality’s assignment and non-assignment review rates?
  2. In high-risk cases, what are the average and longest times from a report to seeing the child?
  3. Where are the regional gaps in abuse examination and forensic capacity?
  4. Which cases require early police and prosecutorial involvement, and who convenes it?
  5. How does government track further reports and repeat victimization after return home or case closure?
