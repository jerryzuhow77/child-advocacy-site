People often ask: “Why not a children's home, a foster family, or adoption by a family in Taiwan?” These are questions worth pursuing, butwithout complete case assessment records, we cannot infer retrospectively that any pathway was necessarily unavailable or portray intercountry adoption as the only way forward.

Another pathway cannot necessarily be taken immediately

Children's homes, foster families and adoption within Taiwan may all be important options, but are not alternatives available at the push of a button. Institutional placement requires assessing the child's age, health and developmental needs, need for intensive care, available beds and professional staff. Foster care requires recruitment, training, assessment and matching, with consideration of trauma, medical care, siblings, schooling and contact with relatives. Adoption involves the necessity of placing the child for adoption, adoptability, adopters' eligibility and suitability, and subsequent support.

Seeing a pathway in hindsight therefore does not establish that it could necessarily have been activated immediately at the time. Nor does this release decision-makers from comparing alternative care options, recording their reasons and continuously protecting the child.What should be examined is whether safety assessments were completed in time when risk appeared, whether alternative arrangements were fully compared, who could escalate decisions, and whether the child was kept safe while waiting and during transitions.

Kaikai and foster families: two forms of care must not be conflated

According to public reporting, while Kaikai awaited adoption matching, the adoption service organization arranged care by a home-based childcare provider. This “home-based care while awaiting adoption” is not the same system as afoster familyarranged by local government under child protection or out-of-home placement procedures. The former sits at the intersection of adoption services and childcare; the latter requires foster family recruitment, whole-family assessment, training, matching, visits and placement management.

The actual care arrangement at the time
Public information refers to home-based childcare during the adoption waiting period; care in a private home cannot simply be called a foster placement.
The foster family system
This is a form of out-of-home placement in which the competent authority assesses the child's needs, the foster family's conditions and suitability, alongside visits, supervision and a placement plan.
The background of insufficient capacity
Difficulty recruiting foster families, complex care needs and stretched resources may explain bottlenecks in the system,but do not directly prove that foster care was sought in this case and no place was available。
Evidence still missing in this case
We need New Taipei City's placement assessment at the time, actual inquiries and matching records, reasons for not using foster or institutional care, and the safety plan for the waiting period.
How to describe the causal chain

What can be confirmed is that “the child was awaiting matching and receiving home-based childcare.” The systemic question is “how this waiting-period care is assessed, supervised and changed when risks emerge.” What cannot yet be confirmed is that “a shortage of foster families meant home-based childcare was the only option in this case.”System congestion is background, not proof of causation in an individual case.

Post-event review should not merely move children from one care label to another. It should make the competent authorities accountable for traceable assessments and arrangements concerning the needs, placement suitability and risks of children awaiting adoption. Whether home-based childcare, foster care or institutional care is ultimately used, clear safety standards, visit frequency, abnormality reporting, supervisory review and alternative arrangements are required.

First clarify: four different pathways

Children's homes / institutional care
One out-of-home placement option, requiring assessment of the child's age, health, needs and available resources.
foster family
Provides temporary family-based care; availability and duration depend on places available at the time, assessment and matching.
Domestic adoption
Requires confirmation of adoptability, a suitable family and legal procedures; waiting and matching should also centre on the child's interests.
Intercountry adoption
Not a lower-standard alternative; it must follow the law, competent authorities' procedures, adoption eligibility requirements and necessary follow-up safeguards.

Questions about pursuing intercountry adoption should go beyond labels

Under the adoption system, matching and subsequent services should centre on the child's best interests. If intercountry adoption was planned, reasonable public scrutiny should ask whether birth-family support and the necessity of adoption were assessed; how domestic matching opportunities, waiting and suitability were evaluated; who cared for the child in the interim; whether each visit recorded verifiable safety observations; and whether injury, medical visits or abnormal care triggered timely escalation, reporting and reassessment.

Why might the system lead to intercountry adoption?

This is not a single step of sending a child abroad. Under the law, when parents or guardians propose adoption because they cannot fulfil their support obligations, matching service providers must first investigate through visits thenecessity of placing the child for adoption. If adoption is assessed as unsuitable, they should provide or refer the family to support, childcare, living assistance or medical services. Adequacy of support for the birth family is the first test.

If adoption is found necessary, the law takes as its principlepriority for adopters within Taiwan. Intercountry adoption is not a simplified replacement for domestic matching; it is a pathway that may be considered only after careful examination of adoptability and matching possibilities within Taiwan. The child's age, health and developmental needs, sibling relationships, acceptable waiting time and whether a suitable family can provide stable long-term care should all be recorded and explained.

International principles are similar: priority should be given to helping children remain with their birth families. Intercountry adoption may become an option when a suitable permanent family cannot be found in the country of origin, with checks on the child's needs, adopters' eligibility and safeguards by the authorities in both places.This is systemic analysis, not a retrospective inference about this case's unpublished assessments.

Four sets of decision records should be disclosed to establish why foster or institutional placement was not used

1. New Taipei City's placement-needs and risk assessment The question is not merely whether a form was completed. Who assessed the need for out-of-home placement, and when? What family, care and safety information was used? If foster or institutional placement was not initiated, what were the reasons, who reviewed them, and when was reassessment scheduled?
2. Foster family and institutional resource records Records should show the actual places available then, whom inquiries were made to, matching conditions and responses. If an option was considered unsuitable, distinguish a mismatch with the child's needs, insufficient resources, excessive waiting, or a failure to make complete inquiries at all.
3. Necessity-of-adoption assessments in 2022 and 2023 How did guardianship, family support and care conditions change between the two procedures? On re-referral, were the necessity of adoption and matching possibilities within Taiwan reassessed rather than carrying forward earlier conclusions? Original assessment reports should establish all of this.
4. The safety plan while awaiting matching An unfinished permanent placement does not justify lower standards for temporary care. Check caregiver selection, visit frequency, supervisory review, records of medical and visible abnormalities, and who verified, reported and handed over warning signs and initiated protection.

The real question is not “which pathway was ultimately chosen,” but whether each unused pathway was actually assessed, with reasons recorded and reviewed. Until complete records are disclosed or lawfully accessed, the eventual pursuit of intercountry adoption cannot be used to infer that foster care, institutional placement or adoption within Taiwan was necessarily impossible.

Decisions recur; safety cannot be assessed only once

A list of options cannot replace a decision record that can be reviewed, questioned and updated. At least three stages should be identifiable:assessment of needs and support before the waiting period, care and visit frequency during that period, and reassessment and escalation when risk signals appear.If any stage records only a conclusion without reasons and handover history, it becomes difficult to establish afterwards whether the child was truly seen.

Continuity of care is itself a safety issue

Public discussion indicates that Kaikai was placed in home-based care while awaiting adoption. This does not establish that other placement options were unavailable; nor should “awaiting adoption” be misrepresented as “having to choose a higher-risk arrangement.” Every arrangement should have traceable risk assessment, decision reasons, supervision and interagency handover.A child's immediate safety must not become secondary while awaiting a permanent family.

This feature's conclusion

We cannot and should not draw conclusions for any organization without the complete, unpublished material. What can be stated clearly is that intercountry adoption is a legally permitted but highly procedural pathway for child care and permanent placement; it does not exclude careful assessment of domestic adoption, foster care or institutional care. Public oversight should require a verifiable basis for each key decision and, when risk signals emerge, put safety ahead of procedures and job titles.

Three things to distinguish when reading about this issue

System principlesexplain how each placement or adoption pathway should be assessed;public, verifiable materialshows which hearings, judgments or official records are available for examination;unpublished individual decision recordscannot be filled in by outside observers. These three cannot substitute for one another, and reasonable questions should not be presented as established responsibility.

Control Yuan investigation: failure was not confined to one stage

Control Yuan investigation report 114 She-Diao 0008 focuses on whether the administrative system upheld the child's best interests, rather than only whether one person saw one warning. Following approval of the report, the Control Yuan issued corrective measures against the Ministry of Health and Welfare and the New Taipei and Taipei city governments; the failures concerning Child Welfare League Foundation social workers were referred to judicial authorities for consideration.This is the purpose of an administrative investigation and corrective action, not a court's determination of an individual's criminal responsibility.

Background:The waiting period is not a blank periodBefore a child enters a permanent family, placement, care, visit and medical information needs even more cross-checking. Each arrangement should record the reasons for selection, the reviewer and the reassessment time.
Process:Procedures cannot replace safetyCompletion of eligibility checks, visits or forms does not necessarily mean risk has been eliminated. Abnormal care, medical visits, changes in appearance or difficulty making contact should berecorded, verified and handed overand escalated when necessary.
Consequences:Supervision must be able to change arrangementsThe focus of reform is to establishwaiting-period risk classification, cross-professional record checks and mandatory review thresholds, allowing immediate changes in care and protection when safety signals appear.

The measure of systemic review should therefore not simply be “whether a visit took place,” butwhether the visit changed the assessment of risk and the next protective action。

Four questions can test whether the system has changed

Reform is not completed by adding a guideline: does risk classification actually change visit frequency? Can agencies cross-check records when abnormalities arise? Who has both the authority and duty to initiate review? Are outcomes followed up after care arrangements change?If every answer is merely “the procedure was completed,” children may still be overlooked within the process.
