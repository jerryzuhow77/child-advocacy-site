KAIKAI CASE COMPANION FEATURE | FAMILY SUPPORT × ADOPTION PLACEMENT × RESTRICTED CONTACT

Grandmother
Outside the Door

She did seek help, and a police report would not itself have brought the child home. Once resources fell short and care was transferred, the address, visits, and updates ran through institutional channels. The family could not simply “go and look.”

The 30-second summary

30-SECOND SUMMARY

Put responsibility back in the right direction

  1. Support gap: Entry into “vulnerable-family” services did not mean full funding for round-the-clock care; ordinary support and the adoption-placement program carried sharply different family costs.
  2. Restricted contact: Under full-time care and adoption-placement services, the grandmother could not freely enter the caregiver’s home or decide visits on her own.
  3. Information asymmetry: The child’s location, condition, visits, and care records were mainly held by the organization, assigned worker, and caregiver.
  4. Domestic adopters already had legal priority: The law in force required domestic priority. Public records do not establish that a qualified Taiwanese family was bypassed or that an overseas adopter had been selected.
  5. The system changed after the case: Local government now enters earlier in assessing adoption necessity, interim care, and visit oversight, but implementation still requires case-level verification.
  6. No reversal of responsibility: After a system limits direct family observation, it cannot blame the family for failing to see what it prevented them from seeing.

Reporting a concern did not automatically return the child; being unable to visit independently does not mean the family failed to try.

CORE ASSESSMENT

It was not that she refused to walk toward the child.
Every route had to pass through someone else.

The grandmother was not outside the welfare system. Taiwan’s Control Yuan states that New Taipei City had opened a vulnerable-family service case. But that service is an assessment-and-referral framework, not an automatic cash benefit sufficient to cover round-the-clock childcare. When ordinary support still left an unaffordable family share while the adoption-placement service could cover the entire caregiver fee, the available options were profoundly unequal.

Under the operation described in the public court summary and hearing material, the grandmother did not have the caregiver’s address or direct contact information after the arrangement began. A visit required coordination through the organization’s assigned worker and the caregiver bringing the child to the organization’s office. She could ask, request a visit, raise concerns, or seek a change; none of those steps guaranteed that she could immediately see or bring home the child.

The more direct contact was restricted, the greater the duty on the organization, visiting worker, supervisors, and competent authorities to see the child independently, cross-check information, and escalate risk.

Evidence boundaryCheckable material points to a funding gap, concentrated communication, and delayed photo delivery. That is not the same as a court finding that the worker deliberately concealed information. This page also does not claim that the grandmother actually contacted police. The full agreement, benefit determinations, visit requests, communications, and casework records still require document-by-document verification.

03 | WHY NOT USE BENEFITS AND KEEP THE CHILD?

The family was in the support system.
The support did not close the care gap.

Taiwan’s “vulnerable-family” service is a framework for intake, risk assessment, and connection to economic, childcare, caregiving, and other support. Opening a case does not mean the government automatically pays the full cost of 24-hour childcare.

Official record

She had already sought help

The Control Yuan confirms that New Taipei City had opened a vulnerable-family case and twice referred the family to adoption-placement services. The question is not whether the family entered the system, but whether the support offered was sufficient to preserve the existing care arrangement.

Hearing material

An unaffordable family share remained

In the caregivers’ trial, the grandmother put the earlier caregiver fee at about NT$30,000 a month. In the worker’s trial, a former welfare-center worker estimated about NT$40,000, expressly said she did not remember the exact figure, and said public resources covered roughly half. These figures cannot be merged into one exact amount; both point to a substantial gap.

Hearing material

The placement program covered the full caregiver fee

The former welfare-center worker testified that ordinary support still left a family share, while the adoption-placement program would cover the full caregiver fee. The grandmother also asked to keep the child with the earlier caregiver and was told that the organization had its own cooperating caregiver.

ConclusionNo law compelled the grandmother to choose adoption. But ordinary family support left a cost she could not bear while the adoption-placement program offered full childcare funding, turning a formal choice into something close to a financial one-way street.

After opening the case, what support did New Taipei actually provide?

Vulnerable-family status is not an automatic entitlement to a fixed payment, but opening a case is not proof of adequate support either. The system should identify which financial, child-living, emergency, and caregiving supports were assessed; which applications were assisted and benefits granted; which were not; and the reasons and records. The available public material does not establish each item, so this page does not claim that New Taipei unlawfully withheld benefits owed to the grandmother. That uncertainty does not remove the question of whether resources could sustain care by relatives and the original childcare arrangement.

Another financial issue: were reductions, installments, and repayment waivers explained?

Prosecution allegation | Reported by news outlets According to TaiSounds (August 27, 2024) and Knews (May 9, 2025), the grandmother wanted caregiver Zhou to continue but faced childcare costs. Prosecutors alleged that the Child Welfare League Foundation’s manual allowed discretionary reductions, installments, or agreed repayment waivers for those unable to repay, yet Chen Shang-jie (陳尚潔) neither informed her nor helped her apply. This was an alleged omission in the indictment, not merely this site’s question.

The financial issue therefore cannot be reduced to “she could not afford care, so a different caregiver or adoption was the only option.” Two responsibilities need separate scrutiny: whether public family support was adequate, and whether the organization explained and helped assess fee adjustments and continuity of care. Families should not be expected to discover unexplained internal options and then blamed for lacking alternatives.

Scope and evidence boundaryThis is a prosecution allegation reported in the news, not a court finding verified by this page or proof of intentional concealment. Reductions, installments, or waivers of childcare repayment are not a government commitment to fund all future care indefinitely, nor a guarantee that an application would succeed. Eligibility, the fee period, what happens if adoption plans end, disclosures, applications, and the court’s assessment must be checked against the manual, agreement, original records, and judgment. An application cannot be assumed to have guaranteed continued care by Zhou or prevented the tragedy.

04 | THE AGREEMENT AND HOW IT OPERATED

What was constrained was the exercise of rights.
A service agreement did not erase every family status.

An adoption-placement service agreement is not a court no-contact order, and signing one does not by itself prove that every kinship or guardianship status disappeared. Yet once the child entered 24-hour care, visits, location, contact, and updates ran through the organization’s process, sharply limiting the grandmother’s practical ability to make contact and verify safety.

05 | WHY NOT CALL POLICE WHEN SOMETHING FELT WRONG?

A report could trigger review.
It did not automatically restore caregiving authority.

She saw filtered fragments

The grandmother was not at the care site and had no direct caregiver contact. Her ability to assess risk depended on what the worker relayed, which photos were supplied, and how delays or anomalies were explained.

A delay in information is documented

Hearing material states that she asked for photos on October 6 and received the September 25 and October 23 sets together on October 24. The worker cited a phone repair and a recordkeeping omission and denied intentional concealment. Intent is not established.

Suspicion is not concrete abuse evidence

Requesting photos or finding an explanation odd is not the same as knowing that a child faces immediate danger. When others hold the location and first-hand records, a relative may struggle to describe the scene, timing, injuries, and urgency to police.

A police report is not a visitation order

A family member with a concrete safety concern can contact police or Taiwan’s 113 Protection Hotline without first proving abuse. That can initiate documentation, reporting, and child-protection review; it does not instantly void the arrangement, authorize entry, or return the child.

No reversal of responsibilityThis does not say police are useless. It explains the steps between a report and seeing the child: locating the child, identifying the lead authority, child-protection reporting, and an official check. A system that deprived the family of direct information cannot later hide its own inspection powers behind “Why didn’t you call sooner?”

06 | WHY WASN’T KAIKAI PLACED WITH A TAIWANESE FAMILY FIRST?

Taiwanese law already required domestic priority.
Priority did not mean immediate placement with the first applicant.

The premise needs correction. The available public record does not show that an overseas family had been selected and a Taiwanese family passed over. Kaikai died while still in full-time interim care, before completed matching, pre-adoptive placement, and court approval; no public evidence identifies a selected overseas adopter.

Confirmed by law

Domestic priority already applied in 2023

Article 16 of Taiwan’s Child and Juvenile Welfare and Rights Protection Act already required priority for domestic adopters. Intercountry adoption was a possible alternative only after no suitable domestic family was found—not an order an organization could simply reverse.

Required process

A child could not simply be “given” to a family

A licensed matching organization had to handle a non-relative adoption. Applicants first underwent preparation, suitability assessment, and review; matching then considered the particular child’s age, health, development, and care needs, followed by gradual contact, living together, and court approval. Domestic priority was a legal ordering rule, not a substitute for suitability and best-interests review.

CWLF’s later account

The case was described as still being prepared for matching

In a 2026 statement, the Child Welfare League Foundation said that, after the adoption plan restarted, stable intent, the child’s development, and possible health or developmental needs still had to be addressed before a family search began. That is the organization’s retrospective account. This site does not have the case matching file and cannot use the statement to verify that every step was timely or complete.

What can be concluded now“Still awaiting matching” cannot be rewritten as “the organization chose an overseas family over a Taiwanese family.” But a domestic-priority rule is not proof that this case completed a timely domestic search. The qualified-applicant list, child-preparation records, matching-review records, domestic-candidate assessments, and any intercountry plan are needed to test whether suitable Taiwanese families were delayed, overlooked, or improperly excluded.

07 | WHAT CHANGED IN TAIWAN AFTER THE CASE?

Public authorities now enter earlier.
Paper reforms still require case-by-case verification.

After the case, Taiwan’s Ministry of Health and Welfare said a private organization should not be left to handle adoption-necessity assessment, matching, interim care, and follow-up oversight on its own. Responsibilities were revised in stages. The measures below were announced or implemented; their existence does not prove that each one is effective in practice.

Local government assesses whether adoption is necessary

Since May 2024, the local government where the child lives must first assess whether adoption is necessary and examine support available to the birth family. The matching organization no longer makes that threshold assessment alone.

Poverty alone must not drive adoption

The ministry expressly said that economic hardship should trigger family and financial support first; poverty alone must not be the reason a child leaves the family. This directly addresses the gap between inadequate ordinary support and full payment inside the adoption-placement program.

Government remains responsible during the wait

If a child needs out-of-home care before matching, the local authority should arrange lawful placement. Local government should also assign a responsible social worker to work with the matching organization rather than withdrawing after referral.

Home-care visits use risk tiers and unannounced checks

Guidance effective in May 2024 calls for unannounced visits at varied times, direct observation of the child, and risk-indicator checks, with stronger attention to children under three and those in round-the-clock or overnight care.

A designated pediatrician adds an independent view

Children under three in vulnerable families, government placements, or adoption proceedings are included in a designated-child-doctor network, reducing reliance on information passed only through the caregiver and a single social worker.

2025 rules codified government roles and an audit trail

The revised regulation requires the organization to notify local government for the necessity assessment and local government to convene matching review and select a suitable adopter. If the birth family withdraws the adoption plan, family support must resume; an intercountry case must also document that domestic-priority procedures occurred first.

What remains unresolved?The Control Yuan found that New Taipei vulnerable-family services, CWLF adoption services, and Taipei home-childcare oversight operated without effective cross-system communication in this case. Annual inspections and evaluation at least every three years already existed before the death yet did not expose the organizational failures in time; those old requirements cannot themselves be counted as post-case reform. Real improvement must be tested by records showing a named case owner, escalation for missed visits, independent checks when relatives cannot make contact, and time-bound cross-jurisdiction closure.

08 | SIX POSSIBLE ROUTES TO HELP

Why could rescue still be difficult under each scenario?

Every route below is a system scenario, not a claim about what the grandmother actually did. The purpose is to show why a relative acting alone would struggle to complete the whole chain: locate, see, verify, change care, and secure immediate protection.

01

Request a visit

System scenario
What she could do

Ask the organization or assigned worker for an in-person visit, photographs, or an update.

Where it could stall

She did not unilaterally control the time, place, address, or approval. A reply or photo was not the same as seeing the child and independently confirming safety.

What the system should do

Log the request and decide it by a deadline. If a visit cannot occur, provide an independent, verifiable safety check.

02

Go directly to the care residence

System scenario
What she could do

Only if she knew the correct address and was lawfully permitted to make contact.

Where it could stall

Without the address or direct contact, kinship alone gave no right to enter a private home. Going there did not guarantee access to or removal of the child.

What the system should do

Those holding the address and inspection authority should intensify checks when warnings arise or contact is blocked.

03

Raise a concern with police

System scenario
What she could do

Describe prolonged lack of contact, conflicting accounts, or another concrete safety concern.

Where it could stall

A police report did not automatically restore caregiving authority or change the arrangement. If treated only as a visitation or adoption dispute, the safety concern might not become a formal check.

What the system should do

Separate family-rights disputes from child-safety concerns; document, report, refer, and confirm who takes responsibility.

04

Contact local social-welfare authorities

System scenario
What she could do

Request a safety check and explain the restricted contact and inconsistent information.

Where it could stall

Across jurisdictions, service systems, and unclear lead responsibility, a relative may not know the right entry point. A referral did not prove that anyone had seen the child.

What the system should do

Use one intake point and record a named owner, deadline, response, and overdue escalation.

05

Ask to end the arrangement or bring the child home

System scenario
What she could do

Raise concerns and request a change from the organization within the service and contractual relationship.

Where it could stall

The agreement, stability of care, placement process, and guardianship arrangements required clarification. A request alone did not create an immediate legal transfer.

What the system should do

Even if care cannot change immediately, safety must be checked independently first. Procedure cannot justify delay.

06

Seek legal assistance

System scenario
What she could do

Ask a lawyer or legal-aid service to clarify the agreement, guardianship, visitation, and available remedies.

Where it could stall

Legal procedures require time, documents, and a clear opposing party. Advice cannot itself produce immediate in-person verification if urgent risk is not simultaneously routed into child-protection review.

What the system should do

Run rights remedies and safety protection in parallel. A family should not have to finish litigation before anyone checks whether a child is safe.

09 | MORE THAN ONE CLOSED DOOR

Six limits combined into breaks in the rescue chain

  1. Could not seeDaily contact ended
  2. Could not locateAddress and direct contact missing
  3. Could not proveFirst-hand records held by others
  4. Could not take homeCare could not be changed unilaterally
  5. Passed between systemsPolice, welfare, and institutional roles differed
  6. Waited for repliesNo timed closure or escalation
A family’s powerlessness should not be read as indifference. The more information and authority a system holds, the less reasonable it is to push responsibility back onto the person left outside.

10 | ASK THE RIGHT QUESTIONS

Replace “Why didn’t the family go?” with four verification questions

Who knew the child’s location?

Were the address, direct contact, and last-minute changes held by only one channel?

Who could see the child without relying on the caregiver’s account?

When relatives could not visit, was an independent visit completed on time?

When was a concern truly accepted?

Not merely “referred,” but assigned to a named person with a deadline and response.

How did blocked contact trigger escalation?

There should be substitute contact, supervisory review, statutory reporting, and public-authority support where necessary.

CONCLUSION

If the system closed the door,
it cannot blame the grandmother for standing outside.

Entry into vulnerable-family services did not mean enough funding for round-the-clock care. Agreeing to adoption-placement services was not giving up the child’s safety. Being unable to bring the child home was not the same as failing to seek help. Where contact itself was restricted, not seeing the harm firsthand cannot become a basis for blaming the family.

The real question is who assumed the duty—after the grandmother could no longer visit freely—to see the child, test inconsistencies, trigger a report, and make sure help arrived.
