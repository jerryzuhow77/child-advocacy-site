03 · EVIDENCE LENS

Four lenses for reading testimony

Day 2 is about not only what was said, but how the witness knew it. These labels distinguish the basis of each statement.

Seen directly

Direct observation

People, objects, locations, or conduct the witness said she observed in person.

Heard

Perception across a space

Sounds the witness reported hearing without seeing the room, including crying, impacts, or equipment.

Record shown

Photos, messages, and floor plans

Counsel used earlier statements, images, messages, and plans to test memory and location.

Not confirmed

Limits of inference and memory

Uncertain dates, matters not seen directly, limited language comprehension, and environmental inferences should be separated from direct observation.

05 · EXAMINATION

How examination tested the testimony

The following summarizes each side's questions and answers. It does not endorse any party's position.

Prosecutor's direct examination

Establishing presence, routine, and observations

Questions covered when the witness began work, where she lived, what spaces she could see, and her observations of the child's food, clothing, standing, bathing, fear responses, and recordings.

  • Ground-floor routine and the child's activity
  • Photos, video, and messages
  • Why the witness considered the situation unusual
Cross-examination by Liu Tsai-hsuan's defense

Testing dates, sightlines, and inference

Questions focused on the hospital stay, certainty about injury dates, whether food preparation was directly observed, and how water temperature and bathroom conditions were inferred without entering.

Cross-examination by Liu Jo-lin's defense

Clarifying language ability and direct observation

Questions confirmed the witness understood little Chinese and could not see the living room from her room, and asked whether she directly saw Liu Jo-lin hit, bind, or punish the child.

Redirect examination

Clarifying blender use, clothing, and filming location

The prosecutor followed up on food-preparation equipment, masks worn by other children, the child's clothing, and the filming position shown in a photo.

Sensitive contentOpen to read categories of alleged mistreatment

The testimony addresses prolonged standing, insufficient clothing, eating and bathing in a bathroom, injuries, crying, and impact sounds. This page does not reproduce injury photos, play audio, or illustrate alleged abuse.

Some matters were seen directly; others were heard through a closed space or inferred from ambient sounds. Cross-examination tested these distinctions.

06 · CITIZEN & PROFESSIONAL JUDGES

What citizen and professional judges asked

After court resumed at 15:05, citizen judges, alternate citizen judges, the associate judge, commissioned judge, and presiding judge asked questions in turn. We group them into eight reading themes concerning presence, scope of observation, and the boundaries between seeing, hearing, and inference.

The eight themes are CPAA's editorial grouping, not a complete list of every question.

01

Days at the home and range of activity

The witness said that from starting work in October 2023 until the grandfather's hospitalization on 9 December, she was at the home for over one week but no more than two weeks each month; while he slept, she cleaned and did housework.

Why it matters | First establish the days and areas in which the witness was present, then assess which routines she may have observed directly.View full Q&A
02

Whether the child bathed at other times

The witness said that if the child did not bathe in the morning, he sometimes bathed in the afternoon; earlier she also said he bathed after eating.

Why it matters | This adds possible bathing times but does not by itself establish a fixed daily routine or frequency.View full Q&A
03

Meals per day and where they were eaten

The witness said that while on the ground floor, the child usually ate a midday meal in the bathroom; when later asked by the associate judge, she said she had not seen him eat dinner.

Why it matters | This describes meals the witness saw and should not be equated with proof that the child ate only once in an entire day.View full Q&A
04

Frequency, duration, and activity while standing

The witness said she saw the child standing almost every day when she was home, usually without moving and sometimes falling asleep while standing; she also said he continued standing after returning from outings.

Why it matters | This tests frequency, movement, and timing and must still be checked against images and other evidence.View full Q&A
05

Clothing, illness, and physical changes

The witness said the child was almost always unclothed, once had a runny nose, and appeared increasingly weak in build and expression between October and 8 December 2023.

Why it matters | Clothing, signs of illness, and bodily changes are the witness's account and must be checked against medical and other evidence.View full Q&A
06

Whether Liu Jo-lin showed concern or assisted

The witness said Liu Jo-lin was present when the child cried during bathing but did not go over, and that she did not see Liu assist while the child stood. On one occasion she saw Liu feed him something but could not confirm it was medicine.

Why it matters | This separates response while present, actual care, and direct conduct; not seeing something does not prove it never occurred.View full Q&A
07

Floor location, plan, and time estimate

Using a floor plan, the witness identified where the child usually stood and where his feet were moved while he lay down. She said she saw him lying in an unlit area while going to retrieve something from the basement. Asked about her earlier estimate of one hour, she answered: “I guessed.”

Why it matters | This tests location, line of observation, and the basis for timing; an unmeasured estimate should not be treated as exact duration.View full Q&A
08

Circumstances when a visitor arrived

The witness said that between October and December 2023 a formally dressed visitor came; it was the first time she saw the child dressed and playing with a toy car, but she could not confirm the visitor was a social worker.

Why it matters | This compares that occasion with her account of ordinary days while preserving the uncertainty about the visitor's identity.View full Q&A
07 · TESTIMONY LIMITS

Four ways to assess testimony and closing submissions

Direct observation

Living conditions, locations, clothing, and interactions the witness said she saw must still be checked against photos, messages, and other evidence.

Sound and environmental inference

Where a closed door prevented a view, the witness relied mainly on sounds or whether equipment was operating; evidentiary weight is for the court.

Limits of memory and inference

Some answers about dates, duration, where food went, or what others saw were uncertain or inferential.

Parties' closing submissions

The prosecutor, both defense teams, and participant counsel offered views on credibility, sightlines, language, direct observation, and differential treatment.
