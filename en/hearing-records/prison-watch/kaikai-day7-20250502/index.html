Seeing forced standing and hearing crying

She said she supported the child as he tipped out, and “fell out three times” in the interview record was not what she meant. She also said “playing dead” merely described Child A falling out.

She said she did not report Child A’s abnormalities to social worker Chen or consult her own social worker, because they discussed only children in her own care. She had reminded Liu Caixuan to tell the social worker.

Direct discrepancy

Frequency of forced standing, presence and intervention

DAY7 | Liu Ruolin

She said she went downstairs only at noon, did not order Child A to stand, and did not know why Mira said he dared not move or play in her care. Prison Watch’s answer field is blank for “Seeing Child A stand for two or three hours, did you take him away?”

Other hearing dates

DAY2｜Mira：She said she saw Child A forced to stand almost every day while at home, sometimes until he fell asleep, and never saw Liu Ruolin help him during it.

DAY4 | Liu Ruolin:She admitted seeing Child A naked and standing, but said she did not know whether it was punishment.

Cross-witness and cross-disciplinary reinforcement

Can “the child self-injures” explain the injuries?

DAY7 | Liu Ruolin

She had no opinion when asked about psychiatric, forensic and pediatric opinions. Day 7 arguments treated the self-injury account as an issue requiring cross-evidence examination.

Other hearing dates

DAY3 | Former carer Hsiao ○-hsiang:She said his limbs were rather tense in infancy, but she saw no self-injury or particular abnormalities in development or care overall.

04-B · SILENCE & UNANSWERED RECORD

Silence and unanswered-record section

“Answer blank,” “defendant silent,” “does not remember,” “photo cannot confirm” and “no opinion” are not equivalent. This section distinguishes them so important boundaries are not flattened in summaries.

Original record blank

Prison Watch DAY7 leaves this answer field blank.

What is known:The public record does not record an answer.

What cannot be inferred:Do not fill it in as denial, admission or silence.

Silence explicitly recorded

The defendant did not answer. The record states that she remained silent.

What is known:The original record explicitly states no answer was given.

What cannot be inferred:Silence itself does not admit a crime or prove an account true.

Time unconfirmed

Photo presentation and timing of the basin incident

The court held that photos alone did not show when they were taken; Liu Ruolin also said she did not remember the hour of the basin incident.

What is known:Photo content and photo timing are separate questions.

What cannot be inferred:Do not complete a timeline from filenames, displayed times or gaps in memory alone.

Memory limits

Bathroom feeding: timing, sounds and exact setting

Answers include “only saw it once,” “don’t know the time,” “no recollection” and having forgotten most of the exact setting.

What is known:The witness admits some direct observation while qualifying details.

What cannot be inferred:Do not expand this into a complete eyewitness account, or erase admitted parts because memory is incomplete.

No opinion

Psychiatric, forensic and pediatric opinions

Liu Ruolin answered “No.”

What is known:She offered no rebuttal when asked about three categories of expert opinion.

What cannot be inferred:“No opinion” does not admit every medical inference or legal attribution.

Procedural limits

A sustained objection does not mean an event did not happen

Rulings that a photo cannot prove timing or cross-examination exceeds scope concern evidence presentation or questioning procedure.

What is known:The court limited that question or method of proof.

What cannot be inferred:A procedural ruling is not itself a final factual judgment on an event.

Four distinct states

Blank = no answer in the public record; Silent = original record explicitly states no response; Does not remember / cannot confirm = limited scope of proof; No opinion = no view offered on a particular question. The four are not interchangeable.

05 · FACT & LAW ARGUMENTS

Three argument routes | How the court understands the same evidence

Prosecution’s arguments

Continuing maltreatment, causation and joint principals

The prosecution argued Child A had been healthy before death and uninjured at handover, and that binding, forced standing, beatings and abnormal meals were repeated and ongoing. Liu Ruolin knew, commented, encouraged, followed up and participated in some acts. It linked death and foreseeability through communications, Mira’s testimony and three doctors’ opinions.

Liu Caixuan’s defense

“The ignored care setting” and unsuccessful requests for help

The defense invoked inadequate handover information, separation anxiety, the child’s special conditions and social-worker responses. It said Liu Caixuan sought help, treated injuries and searched for methods but ultimately responded incorrectly, asking that wrongdoing be distinguished from the moral label “evil.”

Liu Ruolin’s defense

Trust, risk reduction and guarantor status

The defense said longstanding trust between the sisters and limited observation prevented her recognizing abuse; untying, lifting and sleeping beside him reduced risk. It denied shared intent and division of acts necessary for joint principals, and any guarantor status arising from law, contract or assumed care.

Presiding judge’s reminder

The original record states that the presiding judge noted “risk-reducing conduct” was outside the pretrial explanatory instructions, and gave guidance on division of acts for joint principals and guarantor status for omission offenses. This context is retained, rather than presenting only the defense’s definitions.

05-B · FACT / LAW SEPARATION

Separating fact and law

“Did she help?” or “Why didn’t she stop it?” asks different questions at factual and legal levels. This plain-language organization follows Day 7 arguments and the judge’s guidance; it does not supply independent legal conclusions.

Joint principalsShared intent + division of acts
Did the sisters place different actions within one criminal plan rather than acting independently without connection?
It asserted shared intent and division of acts based on joint care, travel among three sites, message comments, knowing non-intervention and some actual handling.
It denied prior conspiracy or shared criminal intent, treating messages as complaints or chat and Liu Caixuan as responsible for primary care and handling.
Which acts are proved, how they connect and whether shared intent formed; sisterhood or presence alone does not establish joint liability.
Guarantor statusA special duty to prevent the result?
Was she more than a bystander, owing intervention, rescue or reporting duties through law, contract or actually assuming care?
It asserted a protective duty from professional childcare experience, joint-care context, control over C and actual assistance.
It said she was not Child A’s contracted carer and had not comprehensively assumed care, lacking duties from law, contract or factual assumption.
Degree of actual care, control over space and child, assumption of protection and legal connection between omission and result.
Risk-reducing conductDid conduct reduce or perpetuate danger?
Did untying, lifting, covering and sleeping beside him actually reduce danger or merely address part of an ongoing risk?
It argued against focusing on one rescue episode, citing knowledge, comments, failure to report and failure to stop it throughout the period.
It said lifting, protecting, untying, taking him to the living room and sleeping beside him reduced immediate risk and should not count as criminal participation.
Whether those acts occurred, how much risk they reduced and what other acts surrounded them; the judge also noted this concept was outside the pretrial instructions.
Do not skip levels

Seeing does not automatically create a legal duty; duty does not automatically mean causing death; helping once does not exclude knowledge or participation at other times. Each level requires corresponding evidence and legal evaluation.

07 · RECONSTRUCTED FULL RECORD

Day 7 procedure and parties’ arguments reconstructed

Prison Watch’s DAY7 record is reconstructed in eight chapters, retaining objections, recesses, each party’s arguments and the presiding judge’s guidance; no separate PDF or plain-text attachment is provided.

01 | Continued hearing: prosecutor questions Liu Ruolin

Using Mira’s testimony, interviews, Exhibit B exchanges and the three routes, the prosecutor asked whether Liu Ruolin saw prolonged forced standing, nakedness, bathroom feeding and cries, and whether she actually helped care. She said she did not order A to stand, went down only at midday and heard cries only once; she admitted bathing/toileting at C and occasional help.

She said “I’ll put my things down, then go beat him” was talk, not a beating. She did not know why Mira said A dared not move or play when left in her care.

02 | Neck scratch, basin and “playing dead”

Liu Ruolin admitted long nails and said A did not cry during changing; she noticed the neck wound only when Liu Caixuan sent a photo. Regarding the basin and “When A came out I put him back” / “fell out three times” in the interview, she said the record did not convey her meaning and she supported A.

The prosecutor further used April 28 testimony to ask about untying, singing and “playing dead.” She said the phrase described A falling out.

03 | Photos, wrapping and medical opinions

The prosecutor showed early-morning photos to ask where and when they were taken. Defense objected; the judge sustained it because photos alone do not establish timing. Liu Ruolin said Liu Caixuan requested the mouth-opening photo at C, denied pinning A down and explained wrapping him awake as safety when going downstairs. She had no opinion on psychiatric, forensic or pediatric views. Recess 09:55; resumed 10:10.

04 | Citizen judges’ questions

No. 3 asked about care experience, discussion of A’s abnormalities with social workers and contact channels. Liu Ruolin said she discussed only her own children with her own worker. No. 6 asked whether Caixuan frightened her: “She is frightening when she loses her temper.” Alternate No. 1 asked about feces and A/C movements; she said she saw feces on his hands and Caixuan held C’s key.

No. 4 asked about neck photos and bloodstains. She was frightened, feared losing foundation assignments and applied medicine the next day. “Not too much freedom” meant routines and rules.

05 | Presiding judge’s questions and defendant’s silence

The judge checked when Liu Ruolin moved to C, who cared for A, and the meanings of “will definitely change,” “hasn’t eaten today” and “his belly disappeared.” For “You can’t outplay him; he has government certification,” the record expressly notes no answer and silence. Citizen judges then withdrew; recess until 10:50 preceded factual and legal arguments.

06 | Prosecution’s factual and legal arguments

Beginning with children’s vulnerability, the prosecution framed cause of death, the defendants’ acts, causation and legal evaluation. It reviewed pre-handover condition, binding, forced standing, beatings, abnormal meals and expert opinions of Hsu Cho-hsien, Lu Li and Chiu Yen-nan in sequence.

It argued maltreatment was repeated and ongoing; Ruolin knew, commented, followed up or failed to stop it. Shared care experience, messages and deleted records supported its claims of joint principals, guarantor status and foreseeable death. These are prosecution arguments, not this page’s findings.

07 | Liu Caixuan’s counsel: factual and legal arguments

Under “The ignored care setting / the far side of the moon,” the defense alleged inadequate handover information: no warning of slow adjustment, stranger anxiety, respiratory infection or bodily tension. It dated discovery of abnormalities, restraints, a friend’s advice to stop caring and social-worker help requests.

It said Caixuan encouraged eating, cared for injuries, asked about dentures and sought religious help, but used misguided improvised methods, asking the court to distinguish wrongdoing and unprofessional care from “evil.” These are defense claims.

08 | Liu Ruolin’s counsel: factual and legal arguments

The defense said longstanding sisterly trust and limited observations prevented a full view of the bathroom and forced standing at A. Lifting, protecting, untying, moving him to the living room, covering and sleeping beside him were argued as risk reduction. The judge noted the concept was outside the pretrial instructions.

It denied prior conspiracy, shared intent and divided acts for joint principals, and legal, contractual or assumed duties for guarantor status. The judge gave additional guidance on participation and law. These defense legal arguments remain for the court to determine.

07-B · FIVE QUESTIONS LEFT FOR JUDGMENT

Five questions remaining at Day 7’s close

Day 7 moved from evidence examination toward legal evaluation. This is not a judgment prediction: available material, remaining disputes and the court’s task are placed alongside each other to show what the court must ultimately answer.

01
Scope of actual care

Across A, B and C, did Liu Ruolin merely help occasionally, or actually participate in care and control?

Available material

C’s key and free access, bathing/toileting, basin handling, photos, lunchtime routes, Mira’s and Caixuan’s testimony.

Still disputed

Access frequency, length of stays, field of view and specific acts included in “occasionally.”

Court’s task

Determine incident by incident whether this was actual care, control or brief assistance only.

02
How much she knew, and when

Did she know forced standing, binding, abnormal feeding and the child’s condition were ongoing?

Available material

Photos/messages, Mira’s daily account, both defendants on DAY4 and DAY7 answers “only once” / “don’t know why she says that.”

Still disputed

Were messages chat, complaints or real acts? Did seeing individual fragments suffice to recognize the overall danger?

Court’s task

Assess subjective knowledge through timing, frequency and context, not a single sentence alone.

03
Intervention or participation

Can untying, lifting and sleeping beside him reduce risk, and how should other messages and non-intervention be evaluated?

Available material

Different basin accounts, interview “put him back in,” defense claims of untying/sleeping beside him, and violent messages.

Still disputed

Fall-out count, putting back, all actions after untying and the relationship between individual help and long-term conduct.

Court’s task

Do not cherry-pick favorable or adverse fragments; determine whether overall conduct reduced, maintained or increased risk.

04
Legal protective duty

In professional childcare and actual assistance, did she owe a special duty to intervene, rescue or report?

Available material

Care background, reporting standards for her own children, no report of A’s abnormalities, C incidents and the sisters’ cooperation.

Still disputed

Contractual duty, factual assumption of care, and which times and locations the duty covered.

Court’s task

Identify the duty’s source before omission, ability to act and responsibility; seeing danger alone does not warrant jumping to conviction.

05
Causation and foreseeability

How do injuries, malnutrition, psychological trauma and death connect to each defendant’s specific conduct?

Available material

Hsu Cho-hsien, Lu Li and Chiu Yen-nan addressed death mechanisms, injuries/care context, psychological trauma and professional limits respectively.

Still disputed

Who performed which acts, which outcomes stemmed from continuing maltreatment, and whether each defendant foresaw death risk.

Court’s task

Medicine explains what happened physically and mentally; law must still determine who is responsible for which acts and outcomes.

Day 7 leaves more than a single conclusion

It leaves five paths requiring separate proof: what was done, what was known, whether it could be stopped, whether a duty existed and how conduct connected to the result.
