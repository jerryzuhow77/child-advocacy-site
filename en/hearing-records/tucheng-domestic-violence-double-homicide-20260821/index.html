Following and killing after a protection-order hearing took two lives. This page does not substitute dramatic language for evidence; it separates prosecution and defense arguments, expert-assessment disputes, sentencing submissions, and the court's official judgment.

Reading focus

The day's arguments focused on three levels: whether the crime was planned and among the most serious; whether the defendant truly lost control and remained capable of rehabilitation; and whether death or life imprisonment met necessity and proportionality requirements.

Prosecution's core positionThe conduct involved complete preparation and purpose, and the defendant continued to externalize blame; it should be assessed as among the most serious crimes.
Victims' core positionThe attack sequence and arrangements for revenge involved cruelty and control, making relatives witness suffering.
Defense's core positionExtremely grave culpability does not necessarily require death; the assessment still identified scope for treatment, and life imprisonment can provide long-term separation from society.
Court's outcomeLife imprisonment on each of two murder counts, with an aggregate sentence of life imprisonment and lifelong deprivation of civil rights.
01

Basic case information

Court / procedure confirmedDate: August 21, 2026; hearing time: 9:30 a.m.; trial format: citizen-judge case. The defendant was Mr. Hsieh; the victims' representative was Attorney Yang; defense counsel were Attorneys Tang, Chen, and Wang. Full names are not disclosed here.

The incident occurred on July 7, 2025. After a protection-order hearing, Mr. Hsieh followed his wife and her younger sister by car, rammed their scooter, then attacked them with a blade and an aluminum rod, killing both.

“In hearing disputes I am like others. What is needed is to ensure there are no disputes.”
Analects, Yan Yuan

Confucius said he was no different from others in hearing litigation, but hoped disputes would cease. Read gently today, this does not ask victims to be silent. It reminds institutions that, beyond determining responsibility after tragedy, they should work to stop danger before it enters a courtroom.

02

Domestic violence, the protection order, and events before the incident

May 17, 2025:Mr. Hsieh had a conflict with his spouse and assaulted her. Ms. Chang called the police and applied to the court for a protection order.

July 6, 2025:Mr. Hsieh went to Ms. Chang's residence to collect belongings and smashed a large ceramic money box nearby. Police referred him for a suspected protection-order violation. Public reports state that the prosecutor released him without bail after further questioning.

July 7, 2025:The court held a hearing on an ordinary protection order. Mr. Hsieh's spouse said that day that she wanted the complainant's representative to accompany her. After the hearing, Mr. Hsieh followed his wife and her younger sister by car, culminating in this incident.

“Tread on frost, and solid ice is coming.”
Book of Changes, Kun

Thin frost is not thick ice, but it is a signal left by the cold. In modern risk management, property destruction, protection-order violations, following, and revenge statements should not be viewed in isolation; together they may trace an escalating danger.

03

Submission by Attorney Yang, the victims' representative

Victims' argumentAttorney Yang stated that after leaving the car, Mr. Hsieh attacked the younger sister rather than his wife first, delivering the most fatal stab to the sister. The victims' side argued that this order was intended to make the wife see her sister die and suffer the loss of a loved one, reflecting intense control, cruelty, and revenge.
“More than a year has passed. Has the defendant really changed?”

The victims' side argued that this was not merely an ordinary killing but the use of death to torment the living, with the character of a cruel killing. According to their submission, Mr. Hsieh had said in message records that he wanted to kill his father-in-law but ultimately did not attack him. They argued that this was not simply abandoning the idea, but leaving the father-in-law alive to suffer the loss of his two daughters.

“Everyone has a heart of compassion.”
Mencius, Gaozi I

Compassion does not decide the legal conclusion. It keeps the court, while discussing punishment, mindful that victims are more than names in a case file. Gentle understanding does not obstruct rigorous proof; both should be present.

04

The prosecutor's “four steps of premeditation”

Prosecution argumentWhether an offense was premeditated can be examined as a whole through four steps: forming the intention, acquiring tools, approaching the targets, and using the tools.
Stage of premeditationMr. Hsieh's explanation
Forming the intention“I only wanted to scare them.”
Acquiring tools“A voice told me to buy them.”
Approaching the targets“I happened to meet you.”
Using the tools“At the time I could not tell the knife from the rod.”

The prosecution argued that although Mr. Hsieh explained the four stages as intimidation, a voice's instruction, a chance encounter, and inability to distinguish tools, his conduct viewed continuously still showed a complete process from intention and preparation through approaching the targets and actual use.

“With preparation, things succeed; without it, they fail.”
Book of Rites, Doctrine of the Mean

The original saying concerns preparation. In court, a maxim alone cannot prove premeditation, but it can help explain the prosecution's reasoning: the court must determine when criminal intent formed by examining a series of concrete preparations and actions.

05

Mr. Hsieh's three explanations and the prosecutor's rebuttals

Mr. Hsieh's explanationProsecution's counterevidence
“I did not intend to kill.”Before the incident, he had placed both sisters on a “one life for four lives” list.
“I could not stop while committing the offense.”During the offense, he could still identify targets, change weapons, and make purposeful movements.
“Afterward my behavior was confused and I was not conscious.”After the incident, he still clearly knew to deal with the weapons.
Prosecution's assessmentClaims of “loss of control” and “no consciousness” conflict with specific actions during and after the incident.
06

On “a voice told me to buy them”

Mr. Hsieh said he bought the tools because “a voice told me to buy them.” According to the psychiatric opinion recounted in court, this was not an auditory hallucination in the psychiatric sense, but closer to a concrete expression of his own thoughts or inner voice.

Mr. Hsieh also said he could not distinguish the knife from the rod at the time. It was pointed out in court, however, that before rushing toward the victims he switched the weapon to his dominant hand. The prosecution argued that switching hands, identifying targets, and changing weapons showed that he had not completely lost understanding and control.

Verification boundary
This passage is an observer's account of expert opinions presented in court, not the complete psychiatric assessment report. Diagnostic terminology and original questions and answers must be checked against the case file.
07

The “one life for four lives” list

Mr. Hsieh: “I did not intend to kill.”
Prosecution argumentBefore the incident, Mr. Hsieh had already placed the sisters on a “one life for four lives” list. This did not fit “I only suddenly wanted to scare them” and might reflect their inclusion as revenge targets before the incident.

The list's admissibility, creation time, and specific contents remain subject to the court's evidentiary examination and findings.

08

Constitutional Judgment 113-Hsien-Pan-8 and “the most serious crimes”

Prosecution argumentThe prosecutor cited Constitutional Judgment 113-Hsien-Pan-8 (2024), stating that the death penalty remains constitutional under exceptionally strict conditions, and argued that this case qualified as among the most serious crimes.

The prosecution used medical amputation as an analogy: a physician's removal of a necrotic limb has medical justification; destroying another person who is treated as a toy to satisfy a desire for control is entirely different, even if both involve removing limbs.

The prosecution also argued that the possibility of more cruel cases elsewhere does not mean this case is not among the most serious. If the possibility of an even worse crime always excluded death, the standard could never be applied.

Legal explanation
Constitutional Court Judgment 113-Hsien-Pan-8 does not declare that death may be imposed for every murder. It requires strict conditions, including individual circumstances among the most serious, complete procedural safeguards, and unanimity of the court. The trial court must determine each condition separately.
“Be as careful at the end as at the beginning, and there will be no failure.”
Tao Te Ching, Chapter 64

The final sentencing step must be as careful as the initial fact-finding. Whether arguing for death or life imprisonment, anger or sympathy alone is insufficient: constitutional boundaries, culpability, and individual circumstances must each be explained.

09

Recess and questions from citizen judges

There was a recess of about ten minutes during the hearing. Afterward, the presiding judge checked whether the citizen judges had further questions.

Though brief, this procedural moment reflects the rhythm of citizen-judge trials. Citizen judges not only hear both sides but can, with judicial guidance, raise matters needing clarification and later participate in deliberation and judgment.

10

Expert report and reoffending risk

According to statements in court, the expert report assessed Mr. Hsieh's overall risk of reoffending as “moderate.” The prosecution argued that differing assessment conditions and technical limitations might have led to underestimating the actual risk.

Prosecution argumentMr. Hsieh was currently in a highly controlled detention-center environment. Good behavior there did not establish that he would perform the same after returning to earlier social relationships and life's pressures outside.
Original wording awaiting confirmation
The observation notes record a phrase approximately like “You cannot use a Ming-dynasty sword / money to behead a Qing-dynasty official.” The exact word and full metaphor await checking against an official transcript or judgment. In context, the prosecution meant that present behavior in detention cannot directly predict risk in a different future setting.

Risk is not only probability

The prosecution compared rain and infectious disease: even a 10% chance of rain may at worst mean getting wet, while a disease with only 1% mortality may still make people fearful of going out. The point was that risk assessment must consider the severity of consequences as well as likelihood.

“In safety, remember danger; in survival, destruction; in order, disorder.”
Book of Changes, Appended Statements II

This does not mean treating a person as dangerous forever. It reminds us to consider environment, triggers, and consequences together. Assessment tools offer one perspective; they cannot replace the full evidence of an individual case.

11

Self-rationalization and externalizing blame

Mr. Hsieh had said, “If they had not appeared that day, nothing would have happened.”
Prosecution's assessmentThis still blamed the victims' appearance rather than truly recognizing that the crime arose from his own choices. The prosecution viewed self-rationalization and externalizing blame as his greatest problems.

The prosecution argued that rehabilitation and reintegration are two-way issues: whether the defendant truly understands responsibility and stops blaming others, and whether society can safely receive him in the future. Real rehabilitation is not merely following rules in detention; it means facing responsibility and reducing danger to others.

“When your actions fail, turn back and examine yourself.”
Mencius, Li Lou I

Modern criminal interventions' emphasis on responsibility has a similar spirit. Rather than blaming others for failure, rejection, or conflict, one must identify one's own choices before asking whether change has begun.

12

Best interests of the children

Prosecutor: “Who left the children without their mother?”

The prosecution argued that Mr. Hsieh's own actions deprived the children of their mother and destroyed their best interests. Those interests should therefore not become a shield for the defendant to avoid death.

Defense viewThe children may not want their father alive now, but their thinking may change as they grow up.
Prosecution responseThe possibility that children might later change their minds or want their father again cannot negate their present views or the harm already suffered.
“Care for your own young, and extend that care to others' young.”
Mencius, King Hui of Liang I

Children are not symbols in a sentencing debate or tools for endorsing either side. Their best interests begin with hearing their current safety concerns, grief, choices, and need for long-term support.

13

The presiding judge questions Mr. Hsieh

Courtroom questions and answersThe presiding judge asked, “Does the defendant have any opinion about the sentence?” Mr. Hsieh answered, “No opinion.”

This reply was brief. These observation notes do not speculate further about his mental state or infer remorse from silence alone. Any assessment must be considered with the whole of his statements, expert opinions, and evidence.

14

Attorney Wang's defense submissions

“Mr. Hsieh deserves punishment—but how much?”
Defense argumentThe defense did not deny that he deserved punishment, but asked the court to distinguish “deserving severe punishment” from “whether death is indispensable.”

The defense described Mr. Hsieh as a husband and father who was gradually excluded from his family, whose self-worth was easily shaken by others' attitudes and family relationships, accumulating resistance, resentment, and frustration. This was the defense's explanation of the background, not a transfer of responsibility to the victims.

15

The defense's pre-incident timeline

May 17:Mr. Hsieh went to his father-in-law's home, later conflicted with and assaulted his spouse. The defense said he had originally wanted to stand up for his wife and wanted her to return and to see his children.

July 2:Mr. Hsieh said he wanted to take the children out, but was refused.

July 5, 23:17:The defense presented Mr. Hsieh eating hot pot alone while crying to describe his loneliness, loss, and broken family relationships.

Purchase of three tools

Defense counsel showed the actual purchase inventory in court, but the observer could not record all the items, prices, and purchase times. This page does not fill them in independently and awaits the judgment or official transcript before supplementing them.

Other internet searches

The defense stated that besides looking up possible consequences of hitting someone, Mr. Hsieh also searched ordinary information such as part-time jobs. Not all search records could therefore be interpreted solely as part of a murder plan.

16

The defense's claim of “emotional collapse”

Defense argumentWhile stopped at a red light, Mr. Hsieh saw the sisters talking and laughing. Comparing that scene with his belief that he “had nothing,” he emotionally collapsed, and impulse ultimately governed the conduct that followed.

The defense also asked professional and citizen judges to be aware of their own emotions during deliberation, lest the brutal outcome, powerful stimuli, and controversy displace judgment of the legal requirements.

Emotional state during a crime can inform the court's understanding of intent formation and degree of responsibility. Emotion does not automatically exclude liability; it must be assessed together with preparation, purposefulness, and subsequent conduct.

17

Attorney Chen's defense submissions

Defense argumentAlthough this was an extremely serious violent crime, it did not necessarily meet the death penalty's requirement of being among “the most serious crimes.”
  • The case also included an attempted-offense component.
  • It was not a killing following prolonged abuse of the victims.
  • Although violent, the methods, the defense argued, did not reach the most extreme brutality among death-penalty cases.
  • A complete murder plan had not formed at a very early stage and then been implemented step by step.

The defense argued that planned murder, as discussed by the Supreme Court, requires an explicit plan formed early and carried out accordingly. Heinous circumstances alone do not directly justify the harshest penalty. If correction and rehabilitation remain possible, imposing death is not necessarily required.

Citing the expert report, the defense said Mr. Hsieh's reoffending risk was moderate and could still decrease with appropriate treatment and corrective intervention.

18

Life imprisonment, parole, and age calculations

The defense noted that a life sentence must be served for more than 25 years before parole eligibility is possible; it does not mean automatic release after 25 years.

Defense calculation's starting pointROC year 115 (2026)
25 years laterROC year 140 (2051), Mr. Hsieh approximately 71
If parole is granted20 years of protective supervision still required
After completing that supervisionApproximately 91 years old

The defense emphasized that Mr. Hsieh does not decide parole himself. The prison must submit the case based on imprisonment and treatment, followed by statutory review that may consider the victims' relatives' views. Authorities can deny parole if rehabilitation is ineffective or risk remains high.

Statutory verification
Under current Criminal Code Article 77, a life prisoner must serve more than 25 years and demonstrate genuine repentance before the prison may request Ministry of Justice approval for parole. Under Article 79, only after 20 years on parole without revocation is the unserved sentence deemed completed. This is a threshold for review, not guaranteed release when time expires.
“There is no greater good than correcting one's errors.”
Zuo Tradition, Duke Xuan, Year 2

The defense used the possibility of rehabilitation to support preserving life; the prosecution questioned whether change was genuine. A modern reading of this saying cannot mean time passing alone: it must include acknowledging responsibility, ending externalized blame, accepting intervention, and withstanding tests in different environments.

19

Defense closing statement

“Judgment is the state's power; protecting children is the state's duty.”

The defense argued that children should retain the possibility of finding answers for themselves when they grow up.

“Life imprisonment lets the father atone for the rest of his life.”

The defense argued that even if the children choose to hate him in the future, Mr. Hsieh should remain alive to personally bear the hatred of the daughter he deeply loves, rather than death ending his possibility of facing responsibility.

This was the defense's value-based argument about the purposes of punishment. The court must independently balance culpability, general and specific prevention, victim impact, rehabilitation prospects, and constitutional requirements.

20

First-instance judgment outcome

Confirmed by New Taipei District Court's August 21, 2026 press releaseThe court delivered a first-instance judgment. The case remained subject to appeal under law.
AssaultThree months' imprisonment.
Violation of a protection order59 days' short-term detention.
Two murder countsLife imprisonment and lifelong deprivation of civil rights for each count.
Aggregate sentenceLife imprisonment and lifelong deprivation of civil rights.

Public reports stated that the victims' relatives were emotional after the verdict and strongly dissatisfied that death had not been imposed. These notes respect their feelings and remind readers that the first-instance judgment was not final; appellate proceedings could follow.

“The people are the state's foundation; with a firm foundation, the state is at peace.”
Book of Documents, Songs of the Five Sons

Law judges one person's responsibility; institutions must learn from the case to protect the next person. Public safety is ultimately not an abstract slogan, but whether someone remains there for a person seeking help after she leaves court and returns to everyday life.
