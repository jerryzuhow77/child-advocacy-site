Consultation is not a diagnosis: connecting youth justice to support

Taoyuan District Court began psychiatric specialist consultation this month. The notice is clear: this is not a formal medical diagnosis; informed consent, privacy and referral are essential safeguards.

What has been verified?

On 11 November, Taiwan Taoyuan District Court announced that, from November 2026, it had begun a psychiatric specialist consultation programme. Its purpose is to help judges and youth investigation/protection officers better understand a young person’s mental and individual needs.

The court expressly states that the service is not a formal medical diagnosis. It provides case-based professional consultation, mental-health education and assessment for young people and parents, with medical or psychological referral where necessary.

Three limits that cannot be skipped

Consultation cannot replace a diagnosis or treatment. A young person is also not merely a source of data: the court identifies informed consent and privacy protection as primary conditions. Cross-disciplinary work must not reduce a young person’s circumstances to an individual deficit; family, school, trauma and access to resources may all matter.

Track whether support actually reaches people

Launching a programme is not evidence of an outcome. The meaningful follow-up questions are whether young people and parents can understand and freely choose consent, whether referrals lead to accessible services, whether waits and handovers work, and how data are kept to the minimum necessary.

The point of linking justice and care is to make support easier to reach—not to apply labels earlier.
