209 high-risk young children received care: how can six hospitals identify child-abuse risk earlier?

Kaohsiung is shifting from waiting for children to arrive at a clinic to proactively reaching families. This is a meaningful development, but 209 is neither a count of abuse cases nor proof that 209 cases of harm were prevented.

Cross-system actionThree cases assessed, referred and followed up

How the network works

The city health bureau identifies child-protection and vulnerable-family cases, extremely-low-birth-weight children, overdue vaccinations, developmental delay and Indigenous-area children as priority groups. Teams conduct home visits covering health, development, daily care and family support; they may involve mental-health and other professionals and connect medical or social-welfare resources.

List matching
Health and social-service lists are compared monthly.
Outreach visits
Medical teams and social workers contact children outside regular care.
Green channel
Urgent medical need can be linked quickly to paediatric care.
Referral follow-up
Medical, psychological and welfare services are connected.

What the published results establish

They establish activity and scale: six hospitals, 209 children receiving care, and three cases completing cross-system referral. Taiwan’s national primary-physician scheme similarly frames care as continuous, proactive case management linking prevention, home visits, screening follow-up and referral.

What they do not establish: 209 is not 209 abuse cases and not 209 prevented cases. The city has not published risk categories, visit frequency, completion rates, time to first contact, reporting outcomes, later safety outcomes or a comparison group. Prevention impact therefore cannot yet be calculated.

What should be tracked next

De-identified reporting should cover time from list matching to first contact, successful and unsuccessful visit rates, completed referrals, green-channel care outcomes, continuation of family support, and—over an appropriate follow-up period—repeat reports, new injuries or loss of contact. Transparency must never compromise a child’s privacy or safety.

Our conclusion: this is suitable for independent tracking as a child-abuse-prevention policy development. The support network has expanded and is operating; service volume should not be presented as successful prevention.
