Trauma-informed justice: protection without giving up a fair trial

Taiwan’s Judicial Yuan has set two improvement directions: review procedural safety and privacy safeguards, and deepen trauma-informed, gender-sensitive training. This is a policy direction—not a case outcome.

On 6 November, Taiwan’s Judicial Yuan announced two directions from its Human Rights, Child Protection and Gender-Friendly Committee: strengthen procedural protections for victims and deepen trauma-informed, gender-sensitive training for judicial personnel.

The notice spans criminal, civil, family, juvenile and administrative proceedings. It refers to measures such as separated questioning and fewer repeated statements in criminal procedure, as well as safety assessment, address confidentiality and closed hearings in relevant family and juvenile proceedings. Existing tools do not prove that every case receives them with equal quality.

What should be tracked?

  • Can children and other victims participate safely without avoidable pressure?
  • Are identifying, location, school and medical details actually protected?
  • Are accessible measures available across regions and language or disability needs?
  • Does training have feedback and measurable practice outcomes—not only attendance hours?
Verification boundary: This is an official meeting resolution and an improvement direction. It is not a new law in force, a ruling in any individual case, or a presumption that either side’s account is true.

Protection and due process must coexist

The Judicial Yuan states that trauma-informed practice does not lower evidentiary standards or replace a defendant’s right to defence. Its stated purpose is to reduce avoidable secondary harm while preserving safety, dignity and a fair process.

Next, public reporting should show how courts implement the measures, whether training reaches frontline practice, and how users’ feedback on safety and access informs improvement.
