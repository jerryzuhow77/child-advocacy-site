Taipei mayoral debate: child-abuse reporting

The Kaikai case shows why a report must become timely protection—not merely a record.

This page summarizes candidates’ public debate statements on 31 October. It focuses on system design and does not decide individual liability or judicial outcomes.

The candidates answered different levels of the problem

Shen Pao-yang focused on consistency, delay and whether cases receive attention only after media or political pressure. Chiang Wan-an answered with measures introduced after major cases. One asked why response speed differs; the other argued that urgent incidents are handled through task forces rather than routine meetings.

Problem frame

Shen Pao-yang

Unequal speedQuestioned whether some cases receive faster action because politicians or media intervene.

Chiang Wan-an

System improvement after harmSaid every mistreatment case is the mayor’s responsibility and each case should drive reform.

Measures

Shen Pao-yang

First 100 daysSuspend or revoke licences once facts are established and immediately support harmed children.

Chiang Wan-an

Existing changesCited three-party joint visits and six visits a year after Kaikai; immediate suspension after a report in another kindergarten case; and unannounced inspections across a chain.

Mayoral leadership

Shen Pao-yang

Routine governanceUsed attendance records for childcare and child-welfare meetings to question sustained oversight.

Chiang Wan-an

Emergency task forcesSaid crises cannot wait for scheduled meetings and that he chaired four Kaikai task-force meetings.

Frontline staff

Shen Pao-yang

Government as a backstopPromised support for social workers and childcare staff, without staffing, supervision or budget figures.

Chiang Wan-an

Direct engagementCited meetings with families and early-education groups, without measurable workload or supervision targets.

Criminal penalties

Shen Pao-yang

Supported childcare legislation and an Executive proposalDid not fully reconcile each disputed legislative vote during the debate.

Chiang Wan-an

Favoured heavier penaltiesAttacked Shen’s voting record. Criminal sentencing is a national-law question, distinct from municipal reporting and protection.

How the Kaikai case was used

Chiang treated Kaikai as the starting point for institutional changes. Shen used child-abuse cases to challenge whether one city applies two speeds of response. These answer different questions—what changed and whether every child receives the same timely response.

Keep two levels separate: harsher punishment addresses what happens after an offence. Reporting, visits, medical confirmation and placement address whether a child is rescued before harm escalates. One cannot substitute for the other.

Where each was most specific—and least complete

Shen was clearest onsuspension, licence revocation, immediate support and a first-100-day priority.
Chiang was clearest onjoint visits, six annual visits, suspension, inspections and four task-force meetings.
Shared gapNeither supplied a complete timeline, escalation threshold, accountable owner, audit method or public outcome measure.

Six questions still unanswered

How many hours may pass between a medical warning, a report and first contact?
Who confirms missed medical care, and when must the case escalate?
Who are the three parties in a joint visit, and what triggers it?
Can contradictory accounts or failure to see the child trigger immediate cross-agency checks?
Are visit, medical and placement decisions recorded on an auditable timeline?
What published data would show that added procedures actually improve safety?

Conclusion: measurable protection, not a contest of concern

Both candidates expressed concern. The useful comparison is whether they provide deadlines, a responsible official, escalation rules and accountability after failure. Kaikai must not be reduced to a debate example; the case should set the minimum standard against delay.

Sources: CNA on the child-abuse exchange, CNA on meetings and task-force management, and The Reporter’s debate review. Candidate claims are distinguished from independently verified facts.
