After Seeing,After Hearing

Recognizing Child Abuse and Taking Safe Action

Seeing begins risk recognition. Hearing begins understanding. Action is how protection begins.

Read on

PROLOGUE

Some calls for help are never spoken

A child walks past us. We may see a mark on their arm without knowing how it happened. We may hear “I’m fine” without noticing the hesitation before those words.

Young children may not have the words to describe harm. Children who live with fear may come to believe that harmful treatment is simply normal life.

Recognizing possible abuse is not about reaching a verdict from one injury or one sentence. It is about pausing when unusual signs recur—looking again, listening longer, and considering whether the child may be unsafe.

Some harm leaves visible marks.
Some calls for help can only be heard in silence.

An incomplete disclosure does not mean nothing happened.
01 / SEE

See the pattern

Do not look only at one injury. Look at the life in which signs keep returning.

No single sign proves abuse. Concern rises when signs recur, several appear together, or explanations do not fit the child’s age, development, or circumstances.

Body and health

  • Repeated or unexplained injuries
  • Injuries inconsistent with the explanation
  • Delayed, refused, or interrupted medical care
  • Marked changes in weight, energy, or development
  • Basic nutrition, hygiene, sleep, or clothing needs repeatedly unmet

Emotion and behavior

  • Sudden fearfulness, vigilance, or startle responses
  • Strong resistance to going home or approaching a person
  • Sudden changes in sleep, eating, or toileting
  • Regression, aggression, or self-harming behavior
  • Extreme compliance or fear of expressing needs

Care and daily life

  • A young child repeatedly left alone or with unsafe care
  • Medical, educational, or safety needs persistently unmet
  • Humiliation, threats, intimidation, or isolation
  • Frequent absence, disappearance, or interrupted care
  • Unreasonable restriction from teachers, clinicians, or others

Environment and family risk

  • Frequent violence or uncontrolled conflict at home
  • Obvious hazards in the living environment
  • Severe caregiver stress without support
  • A rapid decline in the child’s circumstances
  • Similar concerns raised independently by several people
One sign is not a verdict. Repeated, overlapping signs may be an unsigned request for help.
02 / HEAR

Hear the request for help

Listening is more than remembering the words a child used.

A child’s account may be fragmented, inconsistent, or stop halfway. That does not automatically mean it is false. Age, language, fear, shame, trauma responses, and dependence on a caregiver can all affect how a child speaks.

An incomplete account is not an unimportant one.
Sometimes it is everything the child can say right now.
  • Notice hesitation before speaking.
  • Notice fragments that recur.
  • Notice fear of a person or place.
  • Notice sudden retraction, apology, or denial.
  • Notice silence, withdrawal, and physical responses.
03 / RESPOND

When a child tells you

Make the child safer before seeking the whole story.

Helpful responses

“Thank you for telling me.”

“This is not your fault.”

“You do not have to tell me everything at once.”

“I will find someone who can help keep you safe.”

Do not

  • Make the child repeat details
  • Ask leading questions
  • Confront the suspected person in front of the child
  • Promise absolute secrecy
  • Ask the child to collect evidence
Do not ask, “Why did you wait to tell me?”
Ask first: “Are you safe now?”
04–05 / RECORD & ACT

Record facts. Start protection.

Write down dates, times, places, what you directly saw or heard, and the child’s own words. Separate firsthand observations, reports from others, and assumptions.

Check immediate safety

Determine whether the child is being harmed now or faces an immediate threat to life or body.

Document objectively

Record what you actually saw and heard. Do not fill gaps with conclusions.

Seek professional advice

You do not need a complete case file before asking for help.

Report the concern

Share the child’s location, identifying details, timing, frequency, and known risks.

Reporting is not a conviction. It allows professional assessment and protection to begin.

06 / FACTS

Myths must not become reasons for silence

No visible injury means no abuse.Emotional abuse, neglect, coercive restriction, and delayed treatment can also cause serious harm.
An inconsistent account means the child is lying.Age, fear, stress, and trauma can fragment or alter how a child recounts events.
I need evidence before reporting.Members of the public are not expected to investigate. Reasonable concern is enough to seek professional advice.
Reporting means accusing someone of a crime.Reporting starts assessment, protection, and support; it is not a criminal conviction.
AFTER SEEING · AFTER HEARING

After seeing and hearing,
let protection begin.

We may not see the whole story in one injury or hear the whole truth in one sentence. But when unusual signs recur, we can document. When a child speaks, we can listen. When danger may exist, we can seek professional help.
