CHAPTER 1

Case posture and procedural setting

The second-instance case is Taiwan High Court 115-Shang-Su-4688. The 6 November 2026 hearing was a preparatory proceeding focused on defining issues, evidence requests, agency inquiries, and proposed expert assessment—not a final judgment.

The first instance imposed two years’ imprisonment for negligent homicide and acquitted on document forgery. Both sides appealed; the appeal remains pending.

CHAPTER 2

The questions the court must answer

The court must separately identify the source and content of any duty, the information available at each relevant time, Chen’s practical authority and feasible measures, the possibility of preventing the result, and criminal-law causation. A failure in one link cannot be repaired merely by the tragic outcome.

CHAPTER 3

Omission liability and guarantor status

Criminal liability for an omission requires a specific duty to act. The job title “social worker” does not itself establish guarantor status. The inquiry must connect law, contract, assignment, actual undertaking, prior conduct, or another special relationship to this defendant and this child.

Administrative or professional duties may inform the analysis, but they do not automatically become personal criminal liability.

CHAPTER 4

A five-step test for guarantor status

The five connected questions are: source of duty; individualisation of the institutional duty; protective control and actual influence; information sufficient to trigger action; and counterfactual causation.

If Chen was the only person able to contact both the relatives and caregiver at a key moment, that may support an argument about actual influence. It still does not eliminate the need to prove feasible action and a realistic chance of preventing harm.

CHAPTER 5

Feasible action and the chain to prevention

Abstract statements such as “she should have reported” are insufficient. The evidence must show when information was received, what action was legally and practically available, who would receive it, what process could follow, and whether medical or protective intervention could occur before death.

Possible measures include verification, additional visits, facilitating medical care, escalation to a supervisor, reporting, referral, or inter-agency coordination.

CHAPTER 6

Duty of care, warning signs, and reporting

The assessment must return to information available at the time, rather than infer knowledge from the later death. Warning signs need not conclusively prove abuse before they can trigger verification or risk response.

A statutory or professional reporting duty must still be tested for applicable actor, threshold of knowledge, available action, and causal connection to the result.

CHAPTER 7

Institutional division of work

Adoption services, child-protection social work, home-based childcare supervision, social-welfare centres, and caregivers have different powers. Division of labour is neither an automatic defence nor a shortcut to guilt.

For every critical time point, the court should determine who received which information, who could act or escalate, what was done, and whether delay affected risk.

CHAPTER 8

Normative sources and the twelve warning indicators

The origin, version, legal character, intended audience, and availability of the twelve clinical warning indicators at the relevant time must be established. Clinical guidance, administrative directions, professional ethics, internal rules, and criminal duties carry different legal weight.

Later revisions may explain reform but cannot retroactively create a personal duty.

Evidence requests and facts to be proved

Requested materials include guardianship-change files, records from the Wenshan home childcare centre and Shuying social-welfare centre, Ministry of Health and Welfare materials, institutional manuals, assignments, supervision chains, visit records, communications, and escalation procedures.

CHAPTER 10

Legal and social-work expert assessment

Expert input may explain social-work roles, practice standards, and technical causal questions. It must not replace the court’s determination of facts, legal duty, evidentiary weight, or guilt.

Any proposed question should specify the fact to be proved, the material to be examined, and how the answer relates to this defendant’s actual role.

CHAPTER 11

Structure of the parties’ arguments

The defence emphasises institutional boundaries, the need to individualise any duty, limited coercive power, and strict proof of counterfactual causation. The prosecution/first-instance direction emphasises accumulated warning information, actual case responsibility, and available routes to verify, report, seek care, or escalate.

The court must identify which link is already proved, which needs investigation, and which expert questions would merely restate a legal conclusion.

CHAPTER 12

Evidence and reasoning map

Duty source should be mapped to statutes, contracts, assignments, manuals, and supervision. Knowledge should be mapped to dated visit, telephone, message, medical, and inter-agency records. Feasible action should be mapped to authority, resources, reporting routes, and response times.

Only after these links are established can the court assess result-avoidance and objective attribution.

CHAPTER 14

Layered review of rules, roles, and personal duty

The analysis must distinguish verified statutory frameworks, institutional rules still to be obtained, and case-specific facts still to be proved. Institutional obligations become relevant to personal liability only through assignment, supervision, actual work, information, and feasible action.

The Child and Youth Welfare Act, home childcare rules, adoption-service rules, professional standards, and internal manuals must each be used within their proper scope.

Defence and prosecution positions compared

On guarantor status, prior dangerous conduct, Article 53 reporting, service-system boundaries, caregiver guidance, warning indicators, knowledge, feasible action, prevention, and expert evidence, each side advances a different chain of reasoning.
